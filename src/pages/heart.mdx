# Heart disease starts decades before the diagnosis

Atherosclerosis is driven by **ApoB**, the number of atherogenic particles in your
blood, accumulated over _time_: the damage is the area under the curve, particle
concentration multiplied by years of exposure <Citation id="easLdlCausality2017" />.
Measured against _optimal_ targets instead of lab "normal," most adults are already
above the line, even when a standard panel and a 10-year risk score say they are fine.
Two numbers and one decision show where you actually stand.

<h2 id="lpa">Lp(a): check it once</h2>

<LpaTier client:visible />

About 1 in 5 people carry an elevated level, and almost none of them know it. That is the
case for the test in one sentence: a single, cheap draw uncovers a hidden, largely
genetic multiplier that should change how aggressively you manage everything else on this
page, starting with your ApoB and blood pressure.

<h2 id="apob">ApoB: where you actually sit</h2>

Here is the uncomfortable part: "optimal" ApoB is far below what a standard panel treats
as unremarkable. Physiologically normal LDL, the level seen in hunter-gatherers, healthy
newborns, and other mammals that do not develop atherosclerosis, is only about 50 to 70
mg/dL <Citation id="oKeefeOptimalLdl2004" />, which maps to an ApoB well under the 65
mg/dL top of the "Optimal" band below. Typical adult levels run much higher, so most
people who check, including plenty whose lab flagged nothing, land in "High" or "Very
high." ApoB is also a better measure of risk than LDL-C alone <Citation id="snidermanApoB2019" />.

<h2 id="statins">Statins: the highest-leverage lever</h2>

**For most people who check, a statin is worth a serious conversation, not a last
resort.** The benefit is roughly proportional to how much you lower ApoB and for how
long, with no floor found yet <Citation id="fourierVeryLowLdl2017" />, so almost everyone
sits somewhere on that curve where lower is better. Statins are among the most-studied,
safest drugs we have, and they are under-used relative to that benefit. The higher your
ApoB or Lp(a), or the more other risk you carry, the stronger the case, and starting
earlier cuts lifetime exposure instead of reacting to an event. Bring your numbers to
your doctor.

This leans further than current guidelines, which still reserve statins for higher
_calculated_ 10-year risk. It is the prevention-optimal stance, and the "lower for
longer is better" evidence is what supports it.

      people who had previously quit statins over side effects took a statin, a
      placebo, and nothing in random months without knowing which was which:

## Blood pressure

High blood pressure compounds ApoB-driven risk by accelerating arterial damage. "Normal"
is now under 120/80 <Citation id="accAhaBp2017" />. The `130/80` below is the _diagnostic_
line where Stage 1 hypertension begins, not a prevention target; the prevention-optimal
goal is systolic **under 120 if you tolerate it** <Citation id="accAhaBp2025" />. Because
office readings run high, judge yourself against _home_ readings: rested, seated, and
averaged over several days.

## Your 10-year risk, in context

Conventional scores estimate your _next 10 years_. The AHA **PREVENT** model (race-free)
is the current standard <Citation id="preventEquations2024" />. Useful, but it
systematically under-weights younger people, which is exactly why the lifetime exposure
above matters more for prevention.
